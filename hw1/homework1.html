1. Theoretical Background & Problem Statement

In modern blockchain architectures such as Ethereum, public-key cryptography is leveraged to generate account addresses and verify transaction signatures. Ethereum utilizes the elliptic curve secp256k1 over a massive prime field. The core mechanism mapping a Private Key ($k$) to a Public Key ($Q$) is Elliptic-Curve Scalar Multiplication:

Q = k · G = G + G + ... + G (k times)

This operation exhibits one-way function properties: computing $Q$ given $k$ and base point $G$ is computationally straightforward (using algorithms like Double-and-Add in $O(\log k)$ time), whereas deriving $k$ from $Q$ and $G$ requires solving the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP), which is computationally intractable for sufficiently large fields.

Objective: To understand the fundamental mechanics of scalar multiplication without the overhead of 256-bit arithmetic, we study a finite field Toy Curve:

y² ≡ x³ + 7 (mod 17)

2. Mathematical Derivation of Point Addition

Over a prime Galois Field $\mathbb{F}_p$ (with $p=17$), point operations on $y^2 \equiv x^3 + a\cdot x + b \pmod p$ ($a=0, b=7$) are defined algebraically:

  • Point Addition ($P \neq R$): Given $P=(x_1, y_1)$ and $R=(x_2, y_2)$, the slope $m$ and resulting point $S = P + R = (x_3, y_3)$ are:
    m ≡ (y₂ - y₁) · (x₂ - x₁)⁻¹ (mod p)
    x₃ ≡ m² - x₁ - x₂ (mod p)
    y₃ ≡ m · (x₁ - x₃) - y₁ (mod p)
  • Point Doubling ($P = R$): For $2P = (x_3, y_3)$:
    m ≡ (3·x₁² + a) · (2·y₁)⁻¹ (mod p)
    x₃ ≡ m² - 2·x₁ (mod p)
    y₃ ≡ m · (x₁ - x₃) - y₁ (mod p)

Note: All divisions represent multiplication by the modular multiplicative inverse modulo $p=17$.

5. Cryptographic Discussion & Conclusions

  • Public-Key Cryptography (ECDLP): Scalar multiplication on elliptic curves provides pseudo-randomness and non-linearity necessary for security. Crucially, it preserves an additive homomorphic structure: $(k_1 + k_2)G = k_1 G + k_2 G$. This algebraic property allows key exchange protocols (Diffie-Hellman) and digital signatures (ECDSA/Schnorr) to function without exposing private keys.
  • Cryptographic Hash Functions: Unlike ECDLP, cryptographic hash functions (e.g., SHA-256, Keccak-256) require the total destruction of algebraic structure. They strictly adhere to the Strict Avalanche Criterion (SAC), where flipping a single input bit changes every output bit with a probability of 50%, guaranteeing collision resistance and pre-image resistance.

Conclusion: While scalar multiplication exhibits pseudo-random coordinate distributions and an apparent avalanche-like effect across discrete field elements, its underlying algebraic homomorphism makes it fundamentally tailored for Public-Key Cryptography rather than general-purpose Cryptographic Hashing.
