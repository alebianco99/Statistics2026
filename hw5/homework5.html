Homework 5: Numerical Integration (Riemann vs Lebesgue) and Standard Normal CDF Computation

5.1 - Riemann vs. Lebesgue Numerical Integration

1. Theoretical Foundations

The Riemann integral partitions the domain [a, b] into subintervals Δxi and sums the areas of vertical rectangles f(xi*) Δxi. In contrast, the Lebesgue integral partitions the range [ymin, ymax] into measurable subsets and measures the size (preimage length) of domain regions mapped into each vertical strip:

Riemann: ∫ab f(x) dx ≈ ∑i=1..N f(xi*) Δxi
Lebesgue: ∫[a,b] f dμ ≈ ∑j=1..M yj · μ({x ∈ [a,b] | yj ≤ f(x) < yj+1})

Comparative Analysis & Pathological Functions

For continuous and piecewise continuous functions, both Riemann and Lebesgue approaches converge to identical theoretical values. However, for pathological functions like the Dirichlet function:

  • Riemann Integration Fails: Any partition contains both rational and irrational numbers. The upper Riemann sum equals 1 while the lower Riemann sum equals 0. Thus, the Riemann integral does not exist.
  • Lebesgue Integration Succeeds: Because the set of rational numbers Q has Lebesgue measure zero (μ(Q) = 0) and irrationals have measure 1 (μ(R\Q) = 1), the Lebesgue integral evaluates cleanly to 0 · 1 + 1 · 0 = 0.

5.2 - Numerical Computation of the Standard Normal CDF

1. Liouville’s Theorem & The Error Function (erf)

The Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution Φ(z) is defined as:

Φ(z) = (1 / √(2π)) ∫-∞z e-t2/2 dt

By Liouville’s Theorem (and the Risch algorithm), the antiderivative of e-t2/2 cannot be expressed in terms of a finite combination of elementary functions (arithmetic operations, powers, exponentials, logarithms, or trigonometric functions). To provide an exact mathematical representation, special functions such as the Error Function (erf) are introduced:

erf(x) = (2 / √π) ∫0x e-t2 dt   ⇒   Φ(z) = (1/2) [ 1 + erf(z / √2) ]

Numerical Efficiency & Practical Considerations

  • Simpson's Rule (Quadrature): Highly accurate for small z, but requires truncated lower integration bounds (e.g., -10) and incurs computational overhead from repeated exponential evaluations.
  • Power Series Expansion of erf(z): Converges rapidly for small |z| (|z| < 2.5), but requires many terms for larger z due to floating-point truncation and slow tail convergence.
  • Rational Polynomial Approximation (Abramowitz & Stegun / Hart): Computes Φ(z) in O(1) time using minimal multiplications, achieving an accuracy of 10-7 to 10-15 across all real numbers z. This is the industry-standard approach used in low-level math libraries.
