Homework 6: Statistical Independence, Contingency Tables, Chi-Square Test and Causality

1. Theoretical Foundations - Independence in Probability and Statistics

In probability theory, two random variables X and F are defined as independent if their joint probability distribution factorizes into the product of their marginal distributions for all outcomes:

P(X = xi, F = fj) = P(X = xi) · P(F = fj)   ⇔   P(F = fj | X = xi) = P(F = fj)

In applied statistics, we observe finite samples of size N rather than exact theoretical probabilities. Due to random sampling variability, empirical joint relative frequencies (Oij / N) rarely match the exact product of empirical marginals (Ri · Cj / N2). Therefore, we test the null hypothesis of independence (H0) by comparing observed counts Oij against expected counts Eij using Pearson's Chi-Square statistic:

Eij = (Ri · Cj) / N   |   χ2 = ∑i,j (Oij - Eij)2 / Eij   |   df = (r - 1)(c - 1)

3. Interpretation of Results - Association vs. Causality

1. Interpreting Statistical Independence vs. Dependence

When the computed χ2 statistic is large and the corresponding p-value < 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis H0. This indicates that the conditional distribution of F changes significantly depending on the state of X, proving statistical dependence (association). Conversely, a small χ2 value (p-value ≥ 0.05) indicates that observed deviations from Eij are consistent with random sampling noise, supporting statistical independence.

2. Distinguishing Statistical Association from Causality

Establishing statistical dependence between X and F does not imply that X causes F (or vice versa). Correlation and contingency table associations only measure co-occurrence in data. Causal inference cannot be deduced from observational frequency tables alone due to:

  • Confounding Variables (Common Cause): A hidden third variable Z may simultaneously influence both X and F. For example, high ambient temperature (Z) increases both ice cream sales (X) and cooling system thermal stress causing server outages (F), creating a strong statistical association without any direct causal link between X and F.
  • Reverse Causality: A significant χ2 test is symmetric with respect to X and F; it cannot determine whether X → F or F → X.
  • Need for Experimental Control: Proving causality requires randomized controlled experiments or formal causal inference frameworks (such as do-calculus and directed acyclic graphs) to isolate interventions from observational bias.
